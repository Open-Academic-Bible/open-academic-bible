# Preface

My name is Milo Quinn, and I started this project.

As I went deeper into studying the Bible, I kept running into the same discovery: what a passage seems to mean often depends on the translation in your hands. Every translation makes choices, and some of those choices are shaped less by the Hebrew and Greek than by the theological preferences of one tradition or another. Over time those choices can bury what the text meant to the people who first wrote and heard it.

I noticed this most when watching discussions of the Bible online. Someone would build an argument on a verse, and a scholar would reply: "I see, you're reading that translation. But that isn't quite what the text says; the wording there pushes you toward one interpretation." When scholars quote the Bible themselves, they tend to reach for academic, non-confessional translations: ones that read the text in its historical and literary setting and follow the evidence of the manuscripts, even when the result is uncomfortable for a particular doctrine. I came to value those translations, and others from several traditions, including Catholic ones, that take the same approach.

The Open Academic Bible aims to give that kind of translation to everyone, for free. A student should not have to buy a Bible to have a reliable, scholarly text. You may download it, read it, share it, quote it, and print it for your own study, teaching or worship. In time we hope to offer printed editions as well.

## How It Was Made

The translation begins from the Berean Standard Bible, a fresh, careful and public-domain translation. My thanks to everyone who produced it; it was an excellent starting point and needed less adjusting than I expected. Work on this translation began in June 2023, shortly after the Berean Standard Bible was released into the public domain. We then revised it verse by verse wherever its wording departs from the Hebrew and Greek or reads a particular theology into them. Where scholars have questioned how a word or phrase is usually translated, we consulted the major critical commentaries and followed the meaning they support. Where the question remains open, the main alternative is given in a footnote.

For the Old Testament, the base is the traditional Hebrew (Masoretic) text, with readings from the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Greek Septuagint and other ancient witnesses where scholars agree they preserve a better text. For the New Testament, the base is the newest critical Greek text: the sixth edition of the United Bible Societies' Greek New Testament (UBS6), whose text is shared with the coming 29th edition of Nestle-Aland (NA29). This translation is therefore made from the most up-to-date edition of the Greek New Testament there is.

The Gospel of Matthew goes one step further. The editors of the Editio Critica Maior, the major critical edition on which the Nestle-Aland text is built, are completing a new text of Matthew, and this translation already follows their working text. Those changes are not yet in any printed edition of the Greek New Testament, and they may not reach one for years.

The New Testament books also follow the order of the new Greek editions. After the Gospels and Acts come the Catholic Letters (James, Peter, John and Jude), then the letters of Paul, with Hebrews placed after 2 Thessalonians, and finally Revelation. The familiar order in most Bibles goes back to printed editions of the sixteenth century. The new editions return to the order found in most ancient Greek manuscripts, the way the early church itself copied and read these books. Translations and study Bibles will probably take up this order gradually, and in time it is likely to become the standard one.

Every change from the Berean Standard Bible is listed, with its reason, in a separate change log. The footnotes give alternative renderings ("Or ..."), the literal wording of the original ("Heb ...", "Gk ..."), and places where the ancient manuscripts differ.

## What This Edition Contains

Alongside the sixty-six books of the Old and New Testaments, this edition includes two further collections, because a reader who wants to understand the Bible in its own world needs them.

The Apocrypha stands between the Testaments, as it does in academic study Bibles. These are Jewish writings from the centuries around the turn of the era, preserved in the Greek Bible of the early church and counted as scripture by the Catholic and Orthodox churches: Tobit, Judith, the Greek additions to Esther and Daniel, the Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach, Baruch, the books of the Maccabees, 1 and 2 Esdras and others. In this draft they are printed in the World English Bible, a public-domain translation, while our own revision of them is completed.

After the New Testament come the earliest Christian writings that did not become part of it. The first group, "Writings Read with the New Testament," contains books that some early churches copied into their Bibles, listed among the books of the New Testament or read aloud in worship, such as the Didache, the letters of Clement, the Shepherd of Hermas and the Apocalypse of Peter. The second, "Early and Widely Read Writings," contains popular stories, letters and martyr accounts from the same centuries, such as the Protevangelium of James, the Acts of Thomas and the letters of Ignatius. They are included not as scripture but because they show what the first generations of Christians believed, argued about and loved to read, and because the line between what is in the Bible and what is not was drawn later than many readers realize. An introduction at the start of that part explains what is included, why, and how it was translated.

## Two Difficult Choices

Gendered language. Hebrew and Greek often use masculine words for people in general. Where that is clearly the sense, this translation says "person," "people" or "humankind." Where the original word is specifically "father," "son" or "brother," and it is still debated whether a wider meaning is intended, I have kept the word the text uses and given the inclusive alternative in a footnote ("Or parents," "Or child"). I understand both sides of this debate, including scholars who find inclusive renderings fully justified and others who think they go beyond the text. Keeping the original word where the question is open is meant to keep the reader close to the text, not to take a side.

Sexuality. A few words that some translations render as "homosexuals" (for example 1 Corinthians 6:9 and 1 Timothy 1:10) are still debated; their exact meaning in their first-century setting is uncertain. This translation does not use the modern word "homosexual," which the ancient languages do not have, nor does it replace these words with a broad paraphrase. It renders them as closely as possible to what the words themselves say (one of them is formed from "male" and "bed"), and the footnotes say plainly that the meaning is uncertain. I know there is no way to translate these passages that will satisfy everyone. My aim has been to be honest about what the text says and about what we do not know.

Where the context and the evidence are clearer, the translation follows the meaning rather than the word-for-word form, in line with other academic translations.

## A Draft, And An Invitation

This is a draft. I am releasing it now so that people can use it, and I welcome feedback, especially from an academic standpoint: the languages, the manuscripts, the history. I cannot promise to debate every point, but every comment will be read and considered, and it will help improve the translation. The standpoint of this project is academic and non-confessional, so arguments for one theological position are better served by the many fine translations made for that purpose. When the text is ready, I will release version 1.0, and the translation will keep being updated as new scholarship and new editions of the Hebrew and Greek texts appear.

Thank you for taking the time to read this. It has been a pleasure to work on, and I look forward to continuing it.

Milo Quinn
