# Beyond the New Testament

## Beyond the New Testament

### Why these books are here
The New Testament did not arrive as a finished list. For the first three or four centuries, churches in different places read different collections of Christian writings. Some books that are now in the New Testament, such as Hebrews, James, 2 Peter and Revelation, were disputed for a long time; others that did not make the final list were copied into Bible manuscripts, named in early lists of New Testament books and read aloud in worship. The first surviving list that matches today's twenty-seven books comes from Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, in his Easter letter of 367, and even he recommended other books for new believers.
This part of the Bible gathers the most important of those other writings, so that readers can see the wider library of the early church for themselves. Nothing here is presented as scripture, and nothing is presented as heresy. These are the books the first generations of Christians wrote, copied, argued about and loved to read, and many of the beliefs, customs and stories of later Christianity come from them.
### What is included
The first section, *Writings Read with the New Testament*, contains books for which there is early evidence that churches treated them as part of their Bible: they were copied inside Bible manuscripts, named in early lists of New Testament books, or read in church services.
The second section, *Early and Widely Read Writings*, contains books that never had that standing but were among the most widely read Christian writings of their time: stories of Mary and of the childhood of Jesus, accounts of the apostles, and the letters and martyr accounts of the generation that followed them.
Writings that circulated mainly within small separate groups, such as the Gnostic texts found at Nag Hammadi in 1945, are not included. They are important for scholars, but they were not part of the shared reading of the early churches. Later medieval legends are not included either.
### The books in brief
*Didache.* A short church manual: the "two ways" of life and death, and instructions on baptism, fasting, the Lord's Prayer, the Eucharist, travelling prophets and the end of the age.
*1 Clement.* The church in Rome urges the church in Corinth to restore the leaders it has removed and to live in peace and order.
*2 Clement.* The oldest surviving Christian sermon outside the New Testament, calling its hearers to repentance and self-control before the coming judgment.
*Epistle of Barnabas.* A reading of the Jewish scriptures as pointing to Christ and the church, ending with its own version of the two ways.
*Shepherd of Hermas.* Visions of the church as an old woman and as a tower under construction, twelve commandments and ten parables, all centered on the offer of a second repentance.
*Apocalypse of Peter.* Jesus shows Peter the punishments of the wicked and the glory of the righteous; the earliest Christian tour of heaven and hell.
*Letter to the Laodiceans.* A short letter in Paul's name, made largely from phrases of Philippians.
*Acts of Paul.* Three surviving parts: Thecla leaves her fiancé to follow Paul and survives fire and wild beasts; 3 Corinthians defends the resurrection of the body; and Paul dies a martyr under Nero.
*Protevangelium of James.* The birth and childhood of Mary, her marriage to the elderly Joseph, and the birth of Jesus in a cave near Bethlehem.
*Infancy Gospel of Thomas.* Stories of Jesus as a boy, whose wonders astonish, and sometimes frighten, his village and his teachers.
*Gospel of Peter.* The trial, crucifixion and resurrection of Jesus, including a dramatic scene of the risen Lord leaving the tomb followed by a speaking cross.
*Acts of Peter.* Peter's contest with Simon the magician in Rome, the "Quo vadis" encounter with the Lord, and Peter's crucifixion head downward.
*Acts of Thomas.* Thomas is sold as a slave to a merchant from India, preaches a gospel of holiness, and dies a martyr; the book includes the "Hymn of the Pearl."
*Letters of Ignatius.* Seven letters written by the bishop of Antioch on his way to execution in Rome, urging unity around the bishop and insisting that Christ truly had a body and truly suffered.
*Polycarp to the Philippians.* Practical advice and warnings from the bishop of Smyrna, full of echoes of the New Testament letters.
*Martyrdom of Polycarp.* An eyewitness account of the arrest, trial and burning of the eighty-six-year-old bishop of Smyrna.
*Epistle to Diognetus.* A defense of Christianity addressed to an inquirer, describing Christians as the soul of the world.
### How they were translated
Each book was translated afresh from its original language, using the standard published editions of the Greek texts and, where the Greek is lost, the Latin. Older English translations that are free for anyone to use, Kirsopp Lake's (1912) for the Apostolic Fathers and M. R. James's (1924) for the other books, served as a starting point, and every passage was checked against current scholarship for its meaning. Two books survive complete only in other ancient languages: the Apocalypse of Peter in Ethiopic, and 3 Corinthians in Latin, Armenian and other versions. For these the translation follows the scholarly English rendering of those versions and compares it with whatever survives in Greek.
The same principles apply as in the rest of this Bible: plain modern English, inclusive language where the original is clearly speaking of people in general, and footnotes for alternative renderings, the literal wording of the original and places where the manuscripts differ. Many of these books survive in only one or two manuscripts, some of them damaged. Where the text is broken or uncertain, the footnotes say so rather than smoothing over the gap.

## Writings Read with the New Testament

The books in this section were never part of the New Testament as it was finally settled. In the early centuries, though, they were copied inside Bible manuscripts, named in early lists of New Testament books, or read aloud in church services. They are printed here so that readers can see the wider collection the early churches read, with each book's evidence stated plainly.

## Acts of Paul

A second-century narrative of Paul's travels, written in Asia Minor. Tertullian (around 200) says a church elder wrote it "out of love for Paul" and was removed from office for doing so. It appears in the Claromontanus list, and Eusebius counts it among the disputed books. The whole work survives only in pieces; three parts survive intact and are printed here: the story of Paul and Thecla, which was copied and retold for centuries; the exchange of letters known as 3 Corinthians, which was part of the Armenian Bible and was treated as scripture by Ephrem the Syrian in the fourth century; and the Martyrdom of Paul.

## Early and Widely Read Writings

The books in this section were never counted as part of the New Testament, but they were among the most widely read Christian writings of the early centuries. Some shaped Christian art, worship and belief for more than a thousand years; others are the letters and records of the generation that followed the apostles. They are printed here for what they show about early Christian life and imagination, with each book's date and evidence stated plainly.

## Letters of Ignatius

Seven letters written by Ignatius, bishop of Antioch, around 110, while he was being taken under guard to Rome to be executed. Six are addressed to churches in Asia Minor and Rome and one to Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna. They are the earliest witness to a church led by a single bishop with presbyters and deacons, and they argue against teachers who said that Christ only seemed to have a body. The translation follows the original collection of seven, not the expanded and forged letters added later.
